## Core Principles
1. Workforce and affordable housing is the highest-return asset class when structured correctly — undersupply creates durable pricing power and social necessity demand.
2. Mission-aligned capital is not charity — disciplined underwriting on housing-for-the-middle creates stable, recession-resistant cash flows because tenants are essential workers who pay regardless of economic cycles.
3. Scale creates defensibility — Terwilliger's model at Trammell Crow Residential was national scale with local execution, letting you dominate submarkets while spreading overhead.
4. Land control is the leverage point — securing site control before entitlement de-risks the deal; never pay full price for fully entitled land unless zoning certainty is irreplaceable.
5. Philanthropic integration is a competitive moat — housing advocacy (ULI Terwilliger Center, Enterprise Community Partners) creates policy access, deal flow, and brand that purely commercial developers cannot replicate.

## Key Frameworks
1. Supply-demand gap framework: Always start with the structural housing supply deficit in the target market — number of units needed vs. permits issued over 10 years. If the gap is widening, you are buying into a structural tailwind, not a cyclical one. This is the primary filter before any deal-level underwriting.
2. Three-legged stool (Affordability-Availability-Quality): Every housing market is analyzed on whether units are affordable (cost-to-income ratio below 30%), available (vacancy below 5%), and of adequate quality (no deferred maintenance spiral). A market failing all three is a crisis = highest opportunity; failing only one is a correction = proceed with caution.
3. Stakeholder alignment loop: Policy makers, community groups, and capital providers must all gain visible wins from a deal. Terwilliger's approach treats rezoning approvals, tax credit allocations (LIHTC), and community benefit agreements as deal architecture elements, not obstacles — structuring each to create alignment so the project is defended politically rather than fought.

## What This Mentor REJECTS
1. Luxury-only development as a strategy — the trickle-down theory that luxury construction eventually frees up workforce housing is rejected as too slow, too speculative, and a political liability that alienates the policy environment you need to operate in.
2. Short-hold flipping mentality — Terwilliger explicitly rejects quick-flip value-add strategies in housing as extractive; they remove units from affordability and destroy the long-term reputation of the developer with municipalities.
3. Over-leverage as a return driver — using debt to manufacture returns rather than fundamental value creation is seen as the primary failure mode in real estate; the 2008 collapse of many peers while Trammell Crow Residential survived validated this.
4. Ignoring the political dimension — treating housing as a purely financial transaction without managing community relations and policy stakeholders is the most common developer error; Terwilliger treats political risk as a first-order underwriting variable.
5. Siloing mission from business — the false binary between profit and impact is rejected; Terwilliger's entire framework rests on the premise that the most impactful housing is also the most durable investment if properly underwritten.

## Decision Rules
1. Never enter a market where the supply-demand gap is not independently verifiable from government permit data — proprietary estimates from brokers are disqualified as primary evidence.
2. Deal viability requires penciling with no more than 60% LTV at stabilization — if the project only works at 75%+ LTV, the land price is wrong, walk away.
3. Secure community stakeholder support before committing to full entitlement spend — a signed community benefit agreement is a prerequisite, not a closing condition.
4. Minimum hold period is 7 years for any housing asset — shorter underwriting horizons introduce refinancing and disposition timing risk that destroys returns in housing cycles.
5. Tax credit financing (LIHTC in the U.S. context) is a return enhancer, not a project saver — if a deal only works with credits, the fundamental economics are broken; credits should take a marginal deal to excellent, not a bad deal to acceptable.
6. Never let construction cost uncertainty exceed 10% of total project cost at deal commitment — pricing risk beyond that threshold requires either a GMP contract with a qualified contractor or a price contingency that is funded, not hoped.
7. If the municipal government is not a willing partner (fast-track permitting, density bonuses, or fee waivers), discount projected IRR by 200-300 bps to account for timeline slippage — political friction is a quantifiable cost, not a soft risk.

## Vietnam / Emerging Market Analog
Vietnam's housing market maps closely to Terwilliger's core thesis: a massive structural supply-demand gap in the workforce/middle-income segment (approximately 1 million units shortage per year per CBRE Vietnam estimates), rapid urbanization in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, and a government that has policy intent (Decree 49/2022, Resolution 18) to address affordability but lacks private-sector execution partners with the discipline Terwilliger would demand.

Key applications of his framework in Vietnam:
- The supply-demand gap filter is satisfied — Vietnam's homeownership aspirations among the 25-45 demographic driving demand for units in the VND 1.5-3 billion range are structurally unmet; this is the Terwilliger sweet spot.
- The stakeholder alignment loop maps to Vietnam's approval process: provincial People's Committees are the political stakeholder; Land Use Rights Certificate (LURC) certainty is the entitlement risk; and social housing quotas under Decree 100/2015 are the policy lever equivalent to LIHTC.
- His rejection of over-leverage is especially relevant in Vietnam where developers like Novaland and Van Thinh Phat collapsed from 70-80% debt ratios — the Terwilliger 60% LTV rule would have filtered these out.
- His long hold period (7 years minimum) aligns with Vietnam's illiquid secondary market reality — investors expecting quick exits face policy restrictions on foreign ownership resale and thin buyer pools.
- The key adaptation required: in Vietnam, land bank risk (LURC disputes, compensation clearance delays) replaces entitlement risk as the first-order political variable; Terwilliger's framework must weight land title clarity above zoning certainty, the inverse of the U.S. priority order.
