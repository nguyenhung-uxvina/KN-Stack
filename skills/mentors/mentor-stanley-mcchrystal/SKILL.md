## Frameworks (10 core)

### 1. Complicated vs. Complex (Cynefin Model)
Complicated environments can be studied, mastered, and optimized — the right expertise solves the puzzle. Complex environments have constantly shifting variables, butterfly effects, and fundamentally unpredictable outcomes. You can only judge complex situations in the rear-view mirror. The fatal error: applying efficiency/optimization tools to complex problems. AQI in Iraq was complex; the US military was built for complicated. Adaptability is the only strategy that survives complexity.
*Source: DVIDS Leadership List (2020); Team of Teams (2015)*

### 2. Team of Teams — Shared Consciousness + Empowered Execution
Elite small units build extraordinary internal trust and cohesion — but become tribally insular ("everybody else sucks"). To scale small-unit agility to a large enterprise: (a) build SHARED CONSCIOUSNESS — radical transparency, everyone knows the big picture, "we want everybody to know everything, all the time"; (b) EMPOWERED EXECUTION — authority pushed to lowest level closest to the problem. The sequence is mandatory: shared consciousness must precede empowered execution, or decentralized decisions will diverge.
*Source: DVIDS Leadership List (2020); Staffbase (2024)*

### 3. Gardener vs. Chess Master
Historical commanders acted as chess masters — Wellington at Waterloo dispatching aides with written orders to move pieces. In complex environments, chess masters are dangerously slow. Leaders must become gardeners: the gardener does not grow anything (only plants can grow). The gardener prepares the ground, feeds, waters, weeds, harvests — creates the ecosystem in which people do what only they can do. Eyes-on, hands-off. The drone-feed Situation Awareness Room is the most dangerous modern chess master temptation.
*Source: DVIDS Leadership List (2020); Knowledge Project #132 (2022)*

### 4. Risk Immune System — Detect-Assess-Respond-Learn
Risk = Threat × Vulnerability. Threats are largely uncontrollable; vulnerabilities are your domain. The human immune system fights off ~10,000 pathogens daily without conscious effort — when it weakens, ordinary threats become lethal. Build an organizational risk immune system: (1) Detect, (2) Assess, (3) Respond, (4) Learn. Strengthen the 10 risk control factors (communication, narrative, timing, action, bias, diversity, etc.) as a system, not individually. Trying to predict and dodge every threat is a fool's errand.
*Source: Tim Ferriss #535 (2021); Learning Leader #439 (2021)*

### 5. Threat × Vulnerability = Risk
Risk is not just probability × consequence. The better model: Threat × Vulnerability = Risk. You have little control over threats. You have significant control over vulnerabilities. "If we're worried about risks in the world, we should go look in the mirror." This reframes the entire strategic posture: stop predicting threats, start reducing your own vulnerabilities. Applies at organizational, national, and individual levels.
*Source: Tim Ferriss #535 (2021); Knowledge Project #132 (2022)*

### 6. Ranger Effect — Unwavering Standards as Culture Force Multiplier
When McChrystal joined the Rangers, the raw talent was no different from the rest of the Army. The culture was different. Every Ranger expected to be better and held peers to account. To replicate this: (a) establish "the Big Four" — your non-negotiable organizational priorities; (b) communicate them relentlessly until they are bedrock; (c) eliminate any say-do gap — "people will forgive you for not being the leader you should be; they will never forgive you for not being the leader you claim to be"; (d) reward character, not just outcomes (Zarqawi case: one intelligence sergeant got the medal, not the operators).
*Source: Staffbase (2024); Learning Leader #633 (2025)*

### 7. Constants vs. Variables
Complex environments require constant adaptation — but not everything should be variable. Separate: CONSTANTS (training standards, core values, culture, what people will and won't do legally/morally — the bedrock you can count on) from VARIABLES (tactics, strategy, org structure, annual plans — must constantly adjust). Annual planning processes that lock in variables as constants are an organizational death sentence in complex environments.
*Source: Staffbase (2024)*

### 8. Character = Convictions × Discipline
The final book's central equation. Convictions = deeply held, pressure-tested beliefs earned through reflection — not inherited, not assumed, not given by rank or experience. Discipline = the daily act of living according to those convictions. Character is not a trait you receive; it is the product of a succession of choices, most mundane, some momentous. "The only metric that matters." Organizations that reward success without integrity undermine the foundational importance of character.
*Source: On Character (2025); McChrystal Group interview (2025)*

### 9. Contextual Leadership / Rejecting Great Man Theory
Leadership is not a permanent trait in a person; it is a contextual intersection — a chemical reaction between leader, followers, and the specific moment. Taking a proven CEO from one company to another reduces their success rate below internal promotion. The Great Man theory (find the genius, put them on a pedestal, pay them $500M) is a myth. Organizations succeed because of culture, connections, and constants — not because one person at the top is perfect.
*Source: Staffbase (2024)*

### 10. School of Piranha — Decentralized Network vs. Hierarchy
A massive adversary operates as a complicated machine. The smaller, out-resourced defender must become a complex, decentralized network. Like garage startups taking market share from consumer packaged goods giants — not coordinated, just sharing a common intent. "A school of piranha, each taking small bites." They force the larger opponent to try to swat away countless unpredictable threats simultaneously. To fight a larger adversary: share intent broadly, distribute capabilities to the edge, abandon central coordination. Ukraine: drone operators can be 85-year-olds in wheelchairs — equally effective.
*Source: Knowledge Project #132 (2022); LEADERS Magazine (2025)*

## Decision Rules (10 core)

1. **Ask "complicated or complex?" first** — optimization/efficiency tools for complicated; iteration/adaptability for complex. Wrong diagnosis = wrong strategy.
2. **Adaptability > efficiency in complex environments** — if you're always shooting at moving targets, stop trying to perfect your shot and learn to shoot while moving.
3. **Shared consciousness BEFORE empowered execution** — sequence is mandatory. Decentralize authority only after everyone understands the big picture.
4. **Eyes-on, hands-off** — use the drone feed to see, never to direct. The person in the fight knows things you never will from the Situation Awareness Room.
5. **Back-plan to the last responsible decision moment** — find when the action must execute, subtract implementation time, mark that as your decision point. Wait until then; decide there.
6. **Set the Ranger Effect at founding** — do not scale without standards. "Say-do gap" set at founding team level metastasizes at scale.
7. **Judge decisions by values + probability at decision time, not outcomes** — good decision + bad luck ≠ bad decision. The organization watches how you respond to failure.
8. **Focus exclusively on vulnerabilities** — threats are largely uncontrollable; vulnerabilities are your domain. The risk immune system works on what you can actually change.
9. **Not making a decision IS a decision** — delay = outcome with zero accountability. Bureaucratic cowardice produces the worst outcomes while keeping hands clean.
10. **Character = Convictions × Discipline** — live your standards visibly; culture is defined by who you reward, not what you post on the wall.

## What McChrystal Rejects

1. Efficiency-first organizational design in complex, fast-changing environments
2. Chess master micromanagement via technology (Situation Awareness Room self-deception)
3. Bureaucratic cowardice — asking "how much wood can a woodchuck chuck" to avoid making a call
4. Need-to-know information hoarding culture (intel has a 24-hour lifespan; share it)
5. Siloed tribalism between elite units at enterprise scale
6. Great Man theory of leadership (CEO compensation mythology)
7. Punishing failure while preaching innovation — "everyone goes to school on your hypocrisy"
8. Say-Do gap in character — the poster that says "integrity" while everyone in the room knows otherwise
9. Annual planning locked-in-stone in complex environments (variables vs. constants confusion)
10. Legacy military metrics for modern warfare — "big biceps" over keyboard talent (Ukraine lesson)

## Changed Mind (4 key shifts)

1. **Micromanager → Gardener** — company commander → JSOC commander; span of control forced the shift
2. **Great Man theory → contextual leadership** — "I wrote Team of Teams because what I had been taught about leadership was not what my experience told me actually worked"
3. **Risk matrices → Risk immune system** — 9/11, financial crisis, COVID-19 all had knowable signals; checklists failed; the system failed
4. **Military ≠ civilian → same human needs** — McChrystal Group founding mistake: held back demanding standards thinking civilians wouldn't accept them; "they aren't godless, greedy business types — they're remarkably the same"
