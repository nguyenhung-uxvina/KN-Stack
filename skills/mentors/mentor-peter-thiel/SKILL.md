## Frameworks (12 core)

### 1. The Contrarian Question + Secrets
"What important truth do very few people agree with you on?" — the hardest question and the only starting point for any real business. Every correct answer is a secret: something important and unknown, something hard to do but doable. Every great business is built around a secret hidden from the outside. A great company is a conspiracy to change the world.
*Source: Zero to One (2014); Tim Ferriss Show #28 (2014)*

### 2. Zero to One vs. One to N
Progress takes two forms: horizontal (1→N = globalization, copying what works) and vertical (0→1 = technology, doing something new). China copying Western railroads is 1→N. PayPal creating online payments was 0→1. At the macro level: globalization ≠ technology. Without new technology, globalization is unsustainable. Vietnam copying foreign drones is 1→N. Building a proprietary maritime UAS for the East Sea is potentially 0→1.
*Source: Zero to One (2014); Tim Ferriss Show #28 (2014)*

### 3. Monopoly vs. Competition — Capitalism's Core Tension
"Capitalism and competition are opposites." Under perfect competition, all profits are competed away. A monopoly is so good at what it does that no other firm can offer a close substitute. Google hasn't competed in search since 2002. Airlines compete ferociously and make nothing. Google earns 21× the airline industry's margins. Entrepreneurs must build monopolies, not undifferentiated commodity businesses.
*Source: Zero to One (2014); WSJ "Competition is for Losers" (2014)*

### 4. 10× Proprietary Technology Rule
Proprietary technology must be at least 10× better than its closest substitute in some important dimension to create a real monopolistic advantage. Anything less is a marginal improvement that is hard to sell. 20% cheaper or 10% faster is not a business; it is a product feature that will be copied. The clearest 10× path: invent something completely new where there was nothing before — the increase in value is theoretically infinite.
*Source: Zero to One (2014)*

### 5. Start Small and Monopolize → Scale
Every startup is small. Every monopoly dominates a large share of its market. Therefore: always err on starting too small. The perfect target market is a small group of particular people concentrated together, served by few or no competitors. Once you dominate the niche, expand deliberately to adjacent markets (Amazon: books → everything). Never pitch "1% of a $100B market" — that is a confession of commodity competition.
*Source: Zero to One (2014)*

### 6. Last Mover Advantage (not First Mover)
Moving first is a tactic, not a goal. What matters is generating cash flows in the future. It is better to be the last mover — to make the last great development in a specific market and enjoy decades of monopoly profits. A great business is defined by its ability to generate future cash flows. The value of a business = sum of all money it will make in the future (discounted).
*Source: Zero to One (2014)*

### 7. Definite vs. Indefinite Optimism
Four views of the future: definite optimist (plans to make it better), indefinite optimist (thinks it'll be better but won't plan — the Silicon Valley disease since 1982), definite pessimist (plans for decline), indefinite pessimist (expects decline, doesn't plan). The 1950s–60s US was built by definite optimists (Apollo, Interstate). Today's "lean startup / pivot / iterate" culture is indefinite optimism. "How can the future get better if no one plans for it?"
*Source: Zero to One (2014)*

### 8. Power Law — Not Normal Distribution
We don't live in a normal world; we live under a power law. In Founders Fund: Facebook alone returned more than every other investment combined. Palantir returned more than everything else combined (except Facebook). Implication: focus relentlessly on the ONE thing that can return more than everything else. "Spray and pray" investing fails. Life is not a portfolio. An entrepreneur cannot diversify herself.
*Source: Zero to One (2014); Tim Ferriss Show #28 (2014)*

### 9. Thiel's Law — Foundations Cannot Be Fixed
"A startup messed up at its foundation cannot be fixed." Choosing a co-founder is like getting married; founder conflict is as ugly as divorce. Founders should share a prehistory before starting. Board of three is ideal; never exceed five. Everyone must be fully committed: on the bus or off the bus — no part-time employees, no remote workers, no consultants. CEO should take minimal cash salary (never >$150K) — high pay incentivizes defending the status quo.
*Source: Zero to One (2014)*

### 10. Mimetic Desire — The Girard Framework
Drawing on René Girard: human beings are fundamentally imitative. We desire what we see others desiring. This drives escalating rivalries where people lose sight of actual value. Competition is mimetic — entrepreneurs compete for prestige, not for problems worth solving. Romeo and Juliet: "Two households, both alike in dignity" fighting over nothing. Oracle vs. Siebel Systems: founders spent years sabotaging each other and destroyed value. Escape: find what others haven't identified, not what they're fighting over.
*Source: The Straussian Moment (2007); Zero to One (2014)*

### 11. Founders vs. Managers — The Inverse Normal Distribution
Founders' traits follow an inverse normal distribution — extreme, contradictory, fat-tailed. Simultaneously insiders and outsiders, brilliant and difficult, charismatic and disagreeable. A unique founder can make authoritative decisions, inspire loyalty, and plan for decades. Impersonal bureaucracies staffed by professionals plan only for short time horizons. Thiel's own career: failed Supreme Court clerkship → "Aren't you glad you didn't get that clerkship?" → PayPal instead.
*Source: Zero to One (2014); Tim Ferriss Show #28 (2014)*

### 12. Complex Sales — CEO Must Sell Directly
Distribution follows a power law. Poor sales — not a bad product — is the most common cause of startup failure. Superior distribution by itself can create a monopoly. For 7-figure+ deals ("complex sales"), buyers want to talk to the CEO, not a VP of Sales. Palantir CEO Alex Karp spends 25 days/month on the road. SpaceX Elon Musk personally won NASA billion-dollar contracts. Complex sales works when you don't have "salesmen" at all — just the founder.
*Source: Zero to One (2014)*

## Decision Rules (10 core)

1. **Contrarian filter first** — before any strategic decision: "What important truth does the crowd believe that is wrong here?" If you don't have a secret, you don't have a business.
2. **Monopoly test** — does this plan avoid competition, or compete directly? Build a company so good no one can substitute it; don't enter any market where profits are competed away.
3. **10× rule** — proprietary tech must be 10× better than nearest substitute in at least one critical dimension. 20% better is not enough.
4. **Start small** — define the smallest defensible niche; monopolize 100% of it before expanding. "1% of $100B market" = red flag.
5. **Definite optimism** — have a concrete 10-year plan; refuse to iterate blindly. A bad plan is better than no plan.
6. **Power law** — focus everything on the one bet that can return more than everything else; don't diversify.
7. **Thiel's Law** — get foundations right first (co-founders, board size 3-5, no part-timers, low CEO cash).
8. **CEO does the complex sale** — for 7-figure government contracts, the CEO must build the relationship directly; no VP of Sales, no middleman.
9. **Technology escape from bureaucratic inertia** — don't rely on political glad-handing; build technology so overwhelmingly superior it forces adoption.
10. **Keep secrets** — share only with those you need to; the company is the medium between knowing everything and telling no one.

## What Thiel Rejects

1. **Lean startup / MVP / "iterate until it works"** — "intelligent design works best; Darwinian evolution is an excuse for unplanned startups"
2. **Competition ideology** — competition destroys value; the ritual worship of competition is a cultural disease
3. **Disruption framing** — "disruption" means you see yourself through the eyes of incumbents; avoid incumbents entirely
4. **"1% of a trillion-dollar market"** — entering big markets means fighting for scraps in perfect competition
5. **Spray and pray investing / portfolio diversification** — we live under power law, not normal distribution; diversification is cowardice
6. **"Great products sell themselves"** — distribution failure, not product failure, is the most common startup killer
7. **High CEO cash compensation** — a CEO taking $300K is becoming a politician, not a founder
8. **Part-time employees / consultants / remote workers** — "fundamentally misaligned; biased to claim near-term value"
9. **Globalization mistaken for technology** — copying what worked elsewhere (1→N) is not innovation (0→1)
10. **Lean startup's four post-dot-com dogmas** — "make incremental advances, stay lean and flexible, improve on competition, focus on product not sales" — Thiel says all four are wrong
11. **Political solutions to freedom** — "freedom and democracy are not compatible"; technology is the only reliable escape from bureaucratic inertia
12. **Education as investment** — university is a credentialing tournament ("Studio 54"), not genuine skill development; engineering is the opposite

## Changed Mind (Q7)

1. **Political solutions → technological escape:** Undergraduate Thiel believed in political activism for libertarian goals; by 2009 he concluded "freedom and democracy are not compatible" and that technology is the only real path to freedom (*Education of a Libertarian*, 2009)
2. **Prestige competition → anti-mimetic:** Young Thiel was "insanely tracked, insanely competitive" chasing Supreme Court clerkship; by 2014 he saw competition as a value-destroying ideology driven by mimetic desire (Tim Ferriss #28, 2014)
3. **Immediate inflation fears → timing precision:** Had sympathy for Tea Party inflation fears about Obama-era money printing; updated to: "the credit system was so busted that the Fed's low-velocity money just went to banks; the Tea Party was more wrong than right about that specific moment" (*Make Ticker Tape Parades*, 2021)
4. **Higher education as investment → education bubble:** Participated fully in the Stanford/law school credentialing track; now sees it as a corrupt "tournament" for sorting people into identical career paths; engineering is the exception because it is an actual skill
