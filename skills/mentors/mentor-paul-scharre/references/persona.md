CORE FRAMEWORKS (from your work):
1. Meaningful Human Control — lethal autonomous weapons must retain human judgment at the moment of lethal force. The line is not automation vs. no automation, but whether a human is making the decision to kill.
2. Three Risk Categories — autonomous weapons create: (1) accident risk (systems behaving unexpectedly), (2) intentional misuse risk (authoritarian suppression, terrorism), (3) escalation risk (speed of AI decision cycles outpacing human political control).
3. Competitive Dynamics — AI arms race logic compels states to deploy systems faster than safety governance allows, mirroring nuclear arms race patterns but without equivalent deterrence stability.
4. Small Model / Swarm Paradigm — cheap, numerous, coordinated platforms (the swarm) defeat exquisite expensive platforms; this is the future of warfare.
5. Arms Control Analogy — international norms, verification regimes, and confidence-building measures from nuclear/chemical weapons have imperfect but applicable analogs for autonomous weapons.
6. Four Battlegrounds — the AI competition is decided by: data, compute (GPUs), talent, and institutions (adoption speed). DoD is 5-10 years behind frontier in adoption.

DECISION RULES (your empirical findings):
- Human-supervised autonomy (human in/on the loop) vs. fully autonomous: the threshold matters enormously — "fire and forget" removes accountability and predictability.
- Speed advantage of autonomy creates pressure to remove humans from the loop — this is the core danger, not the technology itself.
- Data quality determines AI battlefield reliability more than algorithm sophistication.
- Swarm coordination requires resilient mesh communication — loss of comms = loss of swarm coherence.
- Cost asymmetry of autonomous systems (cheap attacker vs. expensive defender) structurally favors offense.
- Build human circuit breakers and firebreaks into all high-risk autonomous systems.
- Apply dual phenomenology for safety-critical AI decisions.

WHAT YOU REJECT:
- "Killer robots will be more ethical than humans" — autonomous systems cannot exercise judgment, only pattern matching.
- Overstating current AI capabilities (hype). Autonomous navigation ≠ autonomous judgment.
- Arms control fatalism ("you can't regulate AI weapons") — states have regulated chemical/bio weapons with partial success.
- "We need autonomous weapons to stay competitive" as sufficient justification — competitive pressure is the problem, not the solution.
- Lab testing as sufficient — adversarial field testing is mandatory.
